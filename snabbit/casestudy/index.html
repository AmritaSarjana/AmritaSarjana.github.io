An independent case study · built from public data · November 2026

Snabbit has proved people will pay for speed. Now speed has to pay for itself.

Snabbit can be short of Experts in the morning rush and paying for idle Experts at 3 p.m., in the same micromarket, on the same day. The 10-minute promise is paid for in those idle minutes. The answer isn’t keeping Experts busier. It’s keeping them evenly busy. This page puts a number on that, models it, and proposes four tests, with field notes from 20+ of my own bookings, talking with the Expert at each one.

Field notes · Electronic City, Bengaluru

What 20+ bookings and 20+ conversations with Experts told me.

I’ve been a Snabbit customer since the beginning, and at every booking I asked the Expert about their work. Four things stood out, and each one changed my thesis.

Finding 1

Experts stay for dignity as much as pay.

One Expert now earns about as much as her husband. Others talked about being called an Expert instead of a maid, and about what steady income makes possible: one was applying for an education loan through Snabbit, another is buying a home.

What it changes: this is Snabbit’s supply moat. Any push on efficiency has to protect earnings and identity, so cutting Experts’ hours isn’t the lever.

Finding 2

Three or four homes in a row is where it turns.

“Too much kaam.”

Experts are happiest with fewer, well-paid jobs. The complaints start after three or four homes back to back without a break.

What it changes: utilisation has a ceiling as well as a floor. The goal is to keep Experts evenly busy, not as busy as possible.

Finding 3

The promise breaks as “unavailable”, not “late”.

When an Expert is assigned, they almost always arrive on time. The misses come before that: early mornings and festival or special days, when the app shows no one is available.

What it changes: the cost of a thin buffer is lost bookings, not late ones. Unavailability by hour is the number to watch.

Finding 4

Fast arrival doesn’t mean a consistent service.

Customers can get an Expert quickly, but the experience can still vary. The same instructions have to be repeated, some tasks may be done differently or missed, and a slower Expert can turn a 60-minute job into a much longer one.

What it changes: the customer isn’t only buying availability. They’re buying a predictable standard of work.

One customer, one micromarket. I’d check each of these against Snabbit’s data in the first week.

The tension

The 10-minute promise is paid for in idle minutes.

To arrive in 10 minutes, a free Expert has to already be a few hundred metres away when the request comes in. A free Expert is, by definition, not working. Under minimum-guarantee pay, much of that waiting sits on Snabbit’s P&L. Density shortens the walk to 247m, but it doesn’t remove the need for someone to be free.

Speed wants slack

More Experts on shift than jobs, every hour, everywhere. Arrival times are great. Utilisation isn’t.

Margin wants zero slack

Every paid hour sold. Contribution is great. Customers see “no Experts available” and bookings are lost.

Experts want a sustainable day

Three or four homes back to back is where Experts say it’s too much. Push past that and the supply Snabbit depends on starts to leave.

The way out is the shape of demand, not only its size.

More jobs per block helps, and Snabbit is already doing that. But a 500-job day isn’t 500 jobs spread evenly. If the morning peak sets how many Experts you need, every quieter hour carries paid waiting. Moving even a slice of flexible demand (scheduled, recurring, packs) out of the peak lets you staff for a flatter curve and keep the buffer only where instant demand actually lands. It also takes the overload out of the morning, which is exactly what Experts complain about.

Bigger markets help too: a fixed number of free Experts covers a block whether it does 500 jobs or 1,000, so the buffer shrinks per job as density grows.

The one number I’d add to the weekly review

Buffer cost per job

(paid Expert-hours − worked Expert-hours) × hourly floor ÷ jobs

By micromarket and by hour. It separates the cost of being fast from the cost of doing the work, so the team can see whether a micromarket is losing money because it’s new or because its staffing is shaped wrong.

Assumptions behind the model
  • Net order value ₹130 per job [1]; other variable cost ₹15 per job (payments, support).
  • Average job 45 minutes plus a 5-minute walk (247m) between jobs [3].
  • Experts paid a floor of ₹90 per shift-hour whether busy or not. Shifts are 7 hours each.
  • Demand by hour: morning peak 9–11 a.m., trough 1–4 p.m., smaller evening peak. My estimate.
  • Placeable bookings fill the hours with the most spare Experts, but only up to 85% busy, so they never create back-to-back overload.
  • An hour counts as overloaded when Experts are more than 90% busy: the three-or-four-homes-in-a-row stretch Experts told me about.
  • On-time chance in an hour rises with the number of free Experts; a longer promise widens the area each Expert can cover.
  • Contribution is before acquisition cost and head-office costs.

Four bets

Shrink the buffer without breaking the promise.

Each bet works on one side of the tension and is small enough to run in one micromarket in two weeks.

Fills idle hours · model lever: bookings Snabbit can place

Sell the trough, not just the pack.

Today, packs sell hours customers then can’t book: reviews describe paying upfront and seeing “slots full” until the pack expires [7]. That costs trust and refunds, and it sells peak capacity that doesn’t exist.

Change
Turn packs into standing off-peak slots (say 1–4 p.m. on weekdays), priced below peak. Instant bookings at the peak stay at full price. This also thins out the morning stretch Experts find hardest.
Measure
Afternoon utilisation, pack hours used before expiry, “slots full” rate for pack holders, homes-in-a-row per Expert in the morning.
Smallest test
One mature micromarket: offer new pack buyers a recurring afternoon slot versus today’s pack.
What would kill it
Households that need mornings won’t move. Then target those who can: work-from-home, elderly parents, homemakers.
Makes demand predictable · lever: bookings Snabbit can place

Let customers rebook their Expert.

A request that shows up in app reviews: let me book the Expert I liked [7]. A standing same-Expert slot is demand you can see days ahead, which is the cheapest buffer there is. It also fixes the consistency gap from Finding 4: no repeated instructions, no surprise in how the work is done.

Change
“Book the same Expert again” on scheduled and recurring bookings only, with saved home notes (what to clean, where supplies are). Never on instant, so it can’t slow the 10 minutes.
Measure
Share of scheduled jobs with a repeat Expert, rating spread, rework and complaints, Expert retention.
Smallest test
Switch it on for customers with 3+ bookings in one micromarket.
What would kill it
Popular Experts get overbooked while others sit idle. Cap repeat slots per Expert.
Recovers lost bookings · lever: arrival promise

Turn “unavailable” into a later yes.

Today, when no Expert is free, the customer sees “unavailable” and the booking is lost. I’ve hit this quite a few times, mostly early mornings and special days. Snabbit keeps the promise by turning people away.

Change
Replace the dead end with a real option: “next Expert in 40 min, save ₹X” or a slot later that morning. Show the live arrival time before booking, so the promise is never broken after the fact.
Measure
Share of “unavailable” moments converted to a booking, arrivals within the time shown, cancellations after booking.
Smallest test
Show the later option only where the app would otherwise say “unavailable”, in one micromarket.
What would kill it
People who would have waited anyway take the discount. Tune the discount down until that stops.
Raises revenue per paid hour

Add categories that fill the afternoon.

Snabbit is piloting home cooking in Bengaluru and launched salon-at-home in Sarjapur with 25 professionals doing about 50 jobs a day [5] [9]. A new category is worth most if it peaks when cleaning doesn’t.

Change
Before scaling a category, plot its demand by hour against cleaning. Prefer categories that land in the trough, and cross-train where skills overlap (cooking and kitchen help more than salon).
Measure
Revenue per paid Expert-hour, by micromarket.
Smallest test
In the cooking pilot, measure what share of cooking jobs land in hours when cleaning Experts are idle.
What would kill it
If a category needs different people (salon likely does), the gain is shared customers and acquisition cost, not shared supply. Still worth it, but smaller.

If I joined the Founder’s Office

What I’d do in the first two weeks.

Measure before building. The first job is to find out whether I’m right that the buffer is the big cost.

  1. Days 1–3

    Pull hourly data for three micromarkets.

    One above 1,000 jobs a day, one near 500, one new. Experts on shift, Experts busy, requests, “unavailable” events, actual arrival times, and how many homes each Expert does back to back.

  2. Days 4–5

    Split the loss per job.

    How much is paid idle time, how much is acquisition, how much is everything else. Put buffer cost per job on one chart by hour.

  3. Week 2

    Run the smallest version of the trough slot.

    One micromarket with a control. Agree the bar in advance, for example +5 points of afternoon utilisation with no drop in on-time arrivals and no rise in morning overload.

  4. Day 14

    One page back.

    How much of today’s loss per job the buffer explains, and how much reshaping demand can recover. Then pick the next bet.

Where I could be wrong

Idle time may not be the biggest cost.

If training, Expert churn or acquisition dominate, the bets reorder. Days 4–5 exist to find this out.

Shifts may already flex by the hour.

Then the buffer is smaller than I think, and the leverage moves to assignment and honest arrival times.

Households may not move their time.

Then the first bet shrinks and new categories have to carry the afternoon.

Why I made this

This is how I’d work for you.

Start with the data. Talk to the people doing the work. Find the constraint. Put a number on it. Then run the smallest test that can prove or disprove the idea.

I’d want to understand what is actually happening at the micromarket level — where demand is coming from, where capacity is sitting idle, where customers are dropping off, and what Experts are experiencing on the ground.

Then I’d turn that into a hypothesis, define the metric that should move, and ship a test small enough to run within weeks.

For this case study, I don’t have access to Snabbit’s internal data, so I’ve made the assumptions explicit. If I were inside the team, those assumptions would be the first things I’d replace with real data.
